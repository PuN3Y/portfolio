The Birthday Party is a 2025 drama film co-written and directed by Miguel Ángel Jiménez, based on the 2007 novel of the same name by Panos Karnezis. It stars Willem Dafoe, Vic Carmen Sonne, Joe Cole, and Emma Suárez. The film had its world premiere in the Piazza Grande section of the 78th Locarno Film Festival on 7 August 2025.

Premise

[edit]

Greek billionaire Marcos Timoleon hosts a birthday party for his daughter on his private island.[2]

Production

[edit]

Principal photography took place in Corfu and Athens. Filming was completed on 17 August 2024.[3]

Release

[edit]

Bankside Films owns the international sales rights to the film, which was previewed at the European Film Market in February 2025.[4][5] In August 2025, the film was acquired for distribution by Paradiso for the Benelux, Praesens-Film [de] for Switzerland, Hagi Film for Poland, Cinemart for the Czech Republic and Slovakia, Pris for Portugal, Rosebud.21 for Greece, Arthouse Traffic [uk] for Ukraine, Budapest Films for Hungary, MCF [sr] for the former Yugoslavia, CineLibri for Bulgaria, GPI for the Baltics, and Film Clinic for the MENA.[1]

A promotional clip was released on 31 July 2025.[6] The film had its world premiere at the 78th Locarno Film Festival on 7 August 2025.[7] It will also be screened in the Open Air Premiere Programme of the 31st Sarajevo Film Festival in August 2025.[8][9] For its Spain premiere, it was programmed at the 10th BCN Film Fest.[10]

A trailer was released on 5 September 2025.[11] Following the film's theatrical run, it will be launched on HBO Europe and HBO Max.[1]

In April 2026, the film was acquired for distribution in North America by Quiver Distribution.[12] The film is scheduled to be released in selected U.S. theaters, and in video on demand on 5 June 2026.[13] A Contracorriente Films plans to release theatrically the film in Spain on 24 July 2026 under the title El anfitrión.[14]

Reception

[edit]

Peter Bradshaw of The Guardian rated the film three out of five stars, calling it "a strong, confident picture that hits its measured stride early on".[15] Amber Wilkinson of Screen Daily called the film a "stylish tale of hedonism and heartbreak".[16]

Javier Ocaña of El País decried the salad of names, accents, and presences so much in the vein of 1980s and 1990s euro-puddings [fr], some uplifting by Jordana's beautiful cinematography and some musical moments notwithstanding.[17]

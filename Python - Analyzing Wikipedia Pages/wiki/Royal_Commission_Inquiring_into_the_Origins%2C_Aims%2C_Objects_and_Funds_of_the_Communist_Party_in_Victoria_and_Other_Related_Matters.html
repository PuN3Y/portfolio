1949–1950 commission regarding the Communist Party of Australia

The Royal Commission Inquiring into the Origins, Aims, Objects and Funds of the Communist Party in Victoria and Other Related Matters[1] was held before Justice Sir Charles Lowe from 6 June 1949 to 6 March 1950, sitting for 154 days.[2][1]: 5–6  It was to examine the workings of the Communist Party of Australia (CPA), within Victoria. This was in a post-World War II Australian Cold War environment, where the CPA had been banned throughout Australia from June 1940[3] to December 1942.[4]

The commission was precipitated by a series of articles in The Herald newspaper two months prior, led to calls for a national royal commission into the activities of the CPA within Australia,[5] and was followed by events that culminated in the Australian Communist Party v Commonwealth court case.

The Executive Council of Victoria appointed Lowe on 19 May 1949 to be the royal commissioner.[6] Lowe was assisted by Reginald Sholl KC.[7] E. F. 'Ted' Hill represented the CPA, and their leading counsel was Australia's only CPA member ever elected to parliament, Frederick W. Paterson.[7][8][1]: 5 

Terms of reference

[edit]

The inquiry scope was "whether the Communist party by its constitution or propaganda advocates or encourages the overthrow by force or violence of established government".[6][1]: 8–9  Additionally, whether the activities or operations of the party or any of its members have been designed to effect or accomplish—

  1. The overthrow or disruption of representative or democratic institutions;
  2. The subversion of law and order;
  3. The attainment of social, economic, industrial or political ends by force, violence, intimidation or fraudulent practices;
  4. The dislocation or disruption of services safeguarding or essential to the maintenance of the life, health and welfare of the community;
  5. The dislocation, disruption or retardation of industrial production; and
  6. The indoctrination of children and young people with beliefs or ideas calculated or likely to result in activities and operations of the nature referred to.[6]

Principal witness Cecil Sharpley

[edit]

The principal witness was former Victorian executive member of the CPA, Cecil Herbert Sharpley (1908–1985). Sharpley was also a journalist and when the party was declared illegal (1940–1942), he established an illegal press and printing of the party's Guardian newspaper.[9] Sharpley later admitted a leading article in the Guardian represented the party policy to support the Soviet Union and defeat the Menzies Government.[9]

Seeking to denounce the party, it was Sharpley's work with journalists of The Herald that led to the commission.[10][11][12] A seven-article long investigative journalism piece, first being published on 16 April 1949,[13][14] was on the CPA accusing the party of election fraud.[15] For writing the articles, Sharpley later admitted he was to be paid £700 (equivalent to A$58,000 in 2020), and had living expenses also met during the writing period.[16] Part of his claims included union ballot rigging from 1937 over twelve years.[17][18]

He gave evidence for twenty-eight days (over 110 hours in the witness box[19]) and questioned by fourteen counsels.[2]

Imagination

[edit]

Earlier in May 1949 it was said by the Postmaster-General that the Herald articles were drawn from imagination and not objective,[20] and one witness during the inquiry also saying Sharpley's evidence was "imaginary".[21] After a report by Justice Lowe was published in 1950 following the Commission,[22] Sharpley's evidence was found unreliable.[23][17][1]: 7 

Sharpley left Australia and arrived in England on 19 December 1949, and did not return.[24]

Outcomes

[edit]

Released by the start of May 1950, a report of 70,000 words, based on examination of 159 witnesses and 1083 documents,[1]: 5  found "a damning indictment of the activities of a body of men enjoying the advantages of the Australian way of life while plotting to overthrow the country's established institutions and to sabotage its industrial machinery".[25][26]

Communist activist Ralph Gibson (and commission witness) concluded that Justice Lowe, "contrary to our fears and to the Government's hopes, displayed a certain genuine interest in Communist theory and a certain respect for evidence".[27]

Parties

[edit]

Some of the persons involved in the proceedings were:

  • Dick Diamond (1906–1989), communist, state secretary of Actors Equity, and witness
  • Malcolm Henry Ellis (1890–1969), journalist and anti-communist witness[28]
  • Ralph Gibson (1906–1989), communist and former State CPA president, and witness
  • E. F. 'Ted' Hill (1915–1988), communist and counsel, and named in the final section of the report[26][1]: 102–103 
  • Max Julius (1916–1963), communist and counsel assisting E. A. H. Laurie[8]
  • Patricia Kennedy (1916–2012), actress, member of Actors Equity, and witness
  • E. A. H. 'Ted' Laurie (1912–1989), communist and counsel
  • Jack Malvern Lazarus (1917–1992), named in the final section of the report (unfounded allegation)[26][1]: 103 
  • Sir Charles Lowe (1880–1969), presiding judge
  • Samuel Merrifield MLA (1904–1982), Victorian politician, and witness (alleged to have taken part in pre-war communist activities),[29] and named in the final section of the report[26][1]: 101–102 
  • Frederick W. Paterson (1897–1977), communist and leading counsel
  • Cecil Sharpley (1908–1985), trade unionist, former member of State CPA executive, principal witness
  • Reginald Sholl KC (1902–1988), counsel assisting the commission
  • Ronald Grant Taylor, communist solicitor as witness,[30] and named in the final section of the report (unfounded allegation)[26][1]: 101
